Credentialing is the slowest organ in American medicine.

A licensed physician arrives at a hospital, finishes onboarding, and then waits, on average, fourteen weeks to see a patient under their own billing identity. The cost of that wait is not the physician's. It is the hospital's, the patient's, and increasingly, the country's.

Chapter01

The cost of the in-between days.

Definition The "in-between days": the interval between a clinician's hire date and the day a payer accepts a claim under their name.

Every American hospital runs the same chokepoint. A physician is hired. The hospital begins primary-source verification — calls to boards, faxes to NPPES, manual checks of OIG/LEIE — while in parallel pursuing enrollment with every payer that physician will ever bill. Each step depends on the last. The pipeline is serial because the documents are uncertified.

The result, well-documented in the trade press and quietly known by every credentialing director, is a median time-to-bill of 103 days. Three and a half months in which the physician is on payroll, the schedule is half-full, and either patients are routed away or a locum is brought in at a 35–40% premium.

"You don't have a clinician shortage. You have a credentialing throughput problem dressed up as a clinician shortage."

The carrying cost varies by specialty — wider for a procedural subspecialist, narrower for a rural primary-care PA — but the shape is the same: a six-figure loss per physician, per hire, accreting silently in the margin between hire and first claim.

VitalCV does not replace the credentialing process. It replaces the document re-collection at the front of it. The federal evidence — NPPES, OIG/LEIE, PECOS — is asked once, signed, and reused by every downstream verifier. Chapter03

A receipt, not a portal.

Architecture Federal evidence is asked at the source, signed, and reissued as a portable credential the clinician owns.
Definition 01
Credentialing by proxy.

A pattern in which a clinician's federal evidence is verified once, signed, and reused by every downstream verifier — instead of being reasked, re-uploaded, and re-doubted at every employer transition.

VitalCV is, in the most literal sense, a notary for clinician identity. Each time the system asks a federal source a question, it writes a signed receipt: the question, the answer, the timestamp, the source's hash. The receipt is portable. It belongs to the clinician. It is replayable for seven years.

When an employer accepts the clinician, the acceptance itself produces a receipt — an auditEventId, an ISO timestamp, a chain link to the previous record. Months later, a regulator can replay the moment exactly as it happened, against the same federal evidence, on a different machine, with no VitalCV in the loop. That property — verifiability outside the issuer — is the whole product.

This is not a SaaS portal in the credentialing space. It is an infrastructure layer that lets the credentialing portals you already use stop redoing each other's work.

Chapter05

What VitalCV will never do.

Scope discipline The boundary is part of the security model — and the reason auditors will accept the receipts.

A short list, because the absences are part of the architecture:

VitalCV does not store PHI. No patient identifiers, dates of birth, MRNs, or clinical data ever enter the system. The intake form rejects them by validation, not by promise.

VitalCV does not hold passwords. Clinicians sign in through their hospital's identity provider. We do not see, store, or rotate authentication secrets.

VitalCV does not become the source. Federal registries remain authoritative. We sign what they returned; we do not substitute our judgment for theirs.

The discipline is not modesty. It is the reason a regulator months later will accept the receipt: the issuer made no claim it could not back up with the federal source on file.
